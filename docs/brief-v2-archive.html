Research brief · Part 1 of research_and_build

The Decision-Model Recipe

Can a ~400M encoder acquire a general-purpose decision capability that transfers to unseen decision tasks, candidate sets, and schemas with little or no task-specific supervision? Evidence, an experimental design, and the ways the hypothesis could fail.

Prepared for Mingyou Sung · 2026-09-21 · Sources: 3 parallel literature/landscape sweeps, ~100 primary sources (arXiv, ACL, HF model cards, vendor docs, four independent benchmark repos)

Summary

Jev and Laya are not a new capability class. Laya is literally ModernBERT-large with a small option-scoring head, and the zero-shot ability both products sell comes from a training recipe that is published, cheap, and reproducible. One community repo reproduced the full Jev API shape with a 435M DeBERTa for $0.26 of training compute.

The evidence across eight research threads converges on five required ingredients:

  1. A modern encoder backbone. DeBERTa-v3 still beats ModernBERT on accuracy and sample efficiency at ≤512 tokens, but it is a native-512 model; at this brief's 1,024–2,048 token target the evidence favors the ModernBERT family (ModernBERT-large, Ettin-400m, mmBERT). Mamba/SSM backbones are admissible in principle and currently unearning in practice (Section 6).
  2. Single-pass answer-space conditioning: candidate labels injected into the input as marker tokens, one scored position per option, softmax over options. One forward pass regardless of label count.
  3. Format-matched multi-task supervision. This is the active ingredient for zero-shot, and it is diversity of tasks and templates that matters, not data volume. Roughly 30+ task families at a few thousand examples each, with 3+ phrasings per task. LLM-synthetic data is now the standard source.
  4. Calibration as a first-class objective: proper-scoring-rule training (what Jev/Laya call RLCD) or at minimum per-schema temperature scaling. This is the biggest gap in the academic literature and the one place Jev genuinely differentiates.
  5. A few-shot path from the zero-shot checkpoint. Starting adaptation from a format-trained model beats starting from a raw encoder in every measured comparison.

One correction to the framing: the BERT-family's missing zero-shot ability is mostly a training-format gap, not a knowledge-capacity gap. Format-trained encoders at 100–500M match or beat 7B–540B generative models on classification. Pretraining modernity helps, but it is the second-order term.

v2 (2026-09-21): restructured after review around the transfer question. Architecture and training strategy are factored apart (Section 4), a strict generalization ladder replaces loose "zero-shot" claims and re-grades the evidence, including our own first measurements (Section 5), and a factorial experimental program with ablations, pre-registered falsification tests, and a compute budget replaces the recommendation (Section 8).

Section 1Problem framing

The objective as stated: identify what is required to build a decision model that is (a) far lighter than an LLM, (b) outputs softmax probability scores, and (c) needs less fine-tuning data than current BERT-family models such as ModernBERT, whose zero-shot weakness is attributed to lighter structure and less pretraining.

Reframed 2026-09-21 after review: the core question is not which recipe scores best at zero-shot classification. It is: can a ~400M encoder acquire a general-purpose decision capability, state + decision question + candidate options → probability distribution over options, that transfers to previously unseen decision tasks, candidate sets, and schemas with little or no task-specific supervision? The positioning is deliberately modest and stays that way: this is not another architecture wrapped in marketing. First determine how much usable decision capability existing encoder technology yields with better training, better decision conditioning, and less task-specific supervision; stronger claims must be earned by the experiments in Section 8, which include explicit attempts to falsify the proposal.

Made concrete (constraints amended 2026-09-21 after discussion), the target artifact is:

  • Size and serving: ~100–500M parameters, single-GPU or CPU serveable, tens of milliseconds per decision.
  • Architecture: flexible. Transformer encoder, SSM (Mamba-class), and sentence-embedding bi-encoder shapes are all admissible. The recipe itself (head design, training mixture, calibration) must be backbone-agnostic so the backbone stays a swappable component. Section 6 weighs the options against the evidence.
  • Input budget: 1,024–2,048 tokens for state plus question plus options. Laya's shipped 512 (English) / 1,024 (multilingual) limit is a training-config choice sitting on an 8,192-token backbone, and its README documents raising the limit while warning that decision quality at extended lengths is unvalidated. So the target window has to be trained and evaluated at 1–2k, not merely enabled.
  • Output: calibrated softmax probabilities over a request-time answer space (labels or criteria defined in the request, not baked into a head at training time), with useful zero-shot accuracy on unseen tasks.
  • Fine-tuning data target: 10–100 labeled examples per decision option to reach strong task accuracy, against the roughly 500–1,000 per class a vanilla encoder head fine-tune needs on complex tasks. Per-option is the right accounting unit; the literature already reports per-class counts (SetFit 8/class, GLiClass 8/label), so comparisons stay clean, and the 10–100 band sits exactly where measured few-shot gains flatten (100–200 examples per class in every study that reports the curve). For ordinal score outputs read it per level, for binary per side. Two riders: track the per-schema total as well, because imbalanced option sets concentrate labeling cost in rare options; and calibration carries its own label bill (a few hundred held-out examples per schema family for temperature fitting), which can share the fine-tuning pool via cross-fitting but does not disappear.
  • Languages: Phase 1 English, Phase 2 multilingual. No intermediate single-language step. The design consequence lands early: multilingual is a Phase-1 backbone decision even though it is a Phase-2 delivery target, because the recipe transfers across backbones and the weights do not (details in the recommendation).

Framing decisions resolved 2026-09-21: this is general research first (a Potloc use case such as the DA-7547 scope router is a later expansion, not the anchor); the zero-shot bar is comparative and modest (beat Laya's open checkpoints; the program's headline metric is the fine-tuning curve at 10–100 labels per option); the training mixture avoids LLM-synthetic data (human-labeled sets plus procedural generation; Section 7 carries the revised mixture); and open-sourcing is decided after the experiments, so the mixture stays license-clean and API-free to keep that door open. The calibration bar is proposed in Section 11.

Section 2What Jev and Laya actually are

Jev (TypeSafe AI, launched 2026-09-15)

Hosted-only, closed. Takes unstructured program state plus declared questions; returns typed choices, scores, and probabilities in a single non-autoregressive pass. Three primitives (Choice up to 255 options, Score over 2–10 ordinal levels, Noul for P(true)), 64k context, $0.042/M input tokens with free output. TypeSafe discloses no parameter count, no architecture, and no training data; their launch FAQ poses those questions and leaves them unanswered. The training method is named RLCD ("Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions") with no spec published. The "cannot hallucinate" claim is a schema property (outputs are always valid types from the declared space), not an accuracy claim, and TypeSafe says so themselves.

Independent testing is the useful part. Calibration is genuinely good in-distribution (ECE 0.024–0.032 per beri.net) but triples out-of-distribution (0.107), which the testers read as possible benchmark contamination. Zero-shot accuracy is mid-pack: 76.3% on Banking77 vs. 81.3% for the best constrained LLM; 62.6% on a phishing benchmark vs. 81.3% for Claude Haiku 4.5 when asked as a single question. Decomposed into five narrow questions plus a logistic regression fit on 1,000 labels, Jev reached 95.0%, but a fine-tuned small model hit 97.4% with ECE 0.010 on the same benchmark, and a two-line regex managed 91.8%. On unknowable questions it answered at 0.74 mean confidence, and it flagged 0 of 30 out-of-scope messages while reporting 0.99 confidence. Reordering options flips 13% of its choices. Its actual advantages are latency (p50 ~240–280ms hosted) and price (~$0.07 per 1,000 decisions, roughly 3–60x cheaper than constrained LLMs), and TypeSafe concedes it cannot prove the pricing isn't subsidized.

Laya (Convai Innovations, released 2026-09-19, Apache 2.0)

Open weights, and the author documented the mechanism in detail. Backbone is ModernBERT-large (395M) with a two-layer decision head (421M total); the multilingual checkpoint uses mmBERT-base. The input sequence per question is [CLS] <type> question: <instructions> [SEP] [MASK] opt0 [MASK] opt1 ... [SEP] <state> [SEP]. The encoder runs once; hidden states at the option-marker positions are gathered and an MLP scores each to a scalar logit; softmax over that question's options. Multiple questions about one state batch into a single forward pass, ~33ms on a T4. Context is 512 tokens as shipped (1,024 for the multilingual and typed-decisions checkpoints), of which 192–256 are reserved for the question and options; the backbone supports 8,192, and the README shows how to raise the limit while stating plainly that "encoder capacity is 8192 positions; that does not establish decision quality at that length." Confidence is normalized entropy, and calibration is a fitted temperature per (question type, option count), which moved mean ECE from 0.466 to 0.081.

Its RLCD is spelled out: the policy's option logits get zero-mean Gaussian exploration noise (8 samples), reward is a composite of strictly proper scoring rules (log + 0.5·spherical − 1.0·ranked-probability-score), updated by REINFORCE with a group-mean baseline. Training data is described only in prose (support triage, NLI-style pairs, toxicity, real jailbreak prompts, sales conversations; 100% human labels per the author; "13 undisclosed task families" per an independent benchmarker). No dataset names, sizes, or technical report exist.

The load-bearing caveat Laya's own README says to treat it as "a fast base to specialise, not as a zero-shot decision engine." Its base checkpoints score near chance on unseen task families (0.362 vs. 0.318 random on typed-decisions), Banking77 collapses to 0.425 because 77 labels overflow its 192-token question budget, and its headline win over Jev on emotion classification did not reproduce under a frozen protocol (both scored 0.587, p=1.000). Its credible pitch is the architecture plus a cheap fine-tune: 6,000 decisions and 4–5 hours on a free Kaggle 2xT4 beat Jev on the typed-decisions benchmark. Jev, evaluated zero-shot on the same benchmark, was competitive without any of that.

Historical note: this architecture is not new. UniMC (EMNLP 2022) put per-option [O-MASK] tokens in an ALBERT input four years earlier, and Laya's author published the shape in March 2025 (arXiv:2503.23303) before TypeSafe's launch. The 2026 "decision model" wave is productization, not invention.

Section 3Where zero-shot ability in small encoders comes from

Eight research threads (NLI-based zero-shot, TARS, statement-tuning, GLiClass/UTC, instruction-tuned encoders, the PET line, SetFit/FastFit, and the 2025–26 modern-backbone work) agree on the causal story, and three results pin it down:

  • Format supervision is the switch. UniMC ablation: skip the multiple-choice-format training stage and the same backbone drops from 78.6 avg to 38.4, i.e. random chance. The MLM backbone alone contributes almost nothing zero-shot.
  • Task diversity is the dial, and it saturates cheaply. Statement-tuning: widening from 3 to 9 training task families lifts zero-shot average from 26 to 61.2, while 1k examples per dataset already reaches 96% of peak performance. Using 3+ templates per task cuts seed variance from 6.9 to 3.0 std. Laurer's universal NLI models gained +9.4% zero-shot from adding a downsampled ~52k-example classification slice on top of 885k NLI pairs.
  • Capacity is not the binding constraint at this task shape. UniMC at 235M beat T0-11B, FLAN-137B, and PaLM-540B on ANLI and CB. Statement-tuned RoBERTa-large (355M) averaged 75.3 zero-shot on unseen tasks, matching or beating 7B chat models. iPET at 223M beat GPT-3-175B on SuperGLUE with 32 labels. Ettin (JHU 2025), which pretrained paired encoders and decoders on identical data, found encoders win classification at matched size (a 150M encoder beats a 400M decoder on MNLI).

Pretraining modernity matters, but asymmetrically. ModernBERT-Large-Instruct's zero-shot-through-the-MLM-head recipe works on ModernBERT and fails on RoBERTa, which its authors attribute to modern pretraining data mixes. Yet for label-token architectures, DeBERTa-v3 (2021 pretraining) still beats ModernBERT backbones at equal size in both GLiClass's and Laurer's model lines. So the backbone sets the ceiling and the speed; the format-matched multi-task mixture is what buys zero-shot.

The label-injection mechanism separately sets the inference cost shape, and the field has been migrating down that curve. Pass-per-label methods (NLI, TARS, statement-tuning) collapse at high cardinality: measured 52–78x throughput loss going from 1 to 128 labels. All-labels-in-one-pass methods (UniMC, GLiClass, Laya) degrade ~8% over the same range. Embedding-similarity methods (SetFit-style, instruction embeddings) are cheaper still, and a 2026 independent benchmark (BTZSC, 38 models × 22 datasets) found plain embedding models like gte-large-en-v1.5 beat every NLI cross-encoder on zero-shot accuracy while owning the latency Pareto front. Nobody has cleanly benchmarked single-pass label-token models against embedding similarity; that is an open seam.

Earlier drafts compared five bundled "recipes"; that framing conflated the inference mechanism with the supervision that trained it (restructured 2026-09-21 after review). Axis 2: training strategies

Two sub-axes, orthogonal to each other and to Axis 1. Objective: standard cross-entropy on hard labels; CE plus post-hoc temperature (per schema); differentiable proper-scoring losses (log, Brier, spherical, ranked-probability compositions); a contrastive stage (SetFit-style pair construction); RLCD (REINFORCE over noise-perturbed option logits with a group-mean baseline, proper-scoring reward). Supervision source: human-labeled; procedurally generated; LLM-generated (a research-only arm, excluded from any release path under the data policy); mixed.

One analytic note on RLCD before any experiment: every scoring rule in Laya's published reward is a differentiable function of the softmax output, so gradient descent can optimize the same objective directly. The REINFORCE machinery adds gradient variance, and its only plausible contribution is the exploration noise acting as a regularizer. The default hypothesis is that a plain proper-scoring loss matches RLCD (ablations F vs. G below); Jev's RLCD is unspecified, so nothing about it transfers by authority.

Composition note: A1–A3 can each be trained with any objective on the same mixture; that factorial is Section 8's matrix. The prior evidence (Section 3) attaches almost entirely to bundles: SetFit is A2 + contrastive + CE, Laya is A3 + RLCD, Laurer's models are A1 + CE. That bundling is exactly why the earlier comparison could not say where the gains come from.

Section 5The generalization ladder: what "zero-shot" must mean

In most of the literature, "zero-shot" means only that no labeled examples of the test set were seen. That is far too weak for this program's claim. The ladder below replaces it. Every result in this brief, and every future experiment, is graded at the highest rung that can be defended from disclosed training data; undisclosed training data means ungradable and is treated as rung-0 risk.

Re-grading the headline evidence under this ladder:

  • Laurer's 0.676 avg F1: G3–G4 as published, but against BTZSC it is largely G0, since all 17 of BTZSC's source datasets sit in the v1.1 training list.
  • Statement-tuning 75.3 avg at 355M: the cleanest academic G4 evidence in the survey.
  • UniMC over PaLM-540B: G4. TARS: G1–G2 by design. GLiClass 0.719: mixture partially undisclosed, ungradable beyond G3.
  • Laya: spam, support, NLI, and toxicity are its training families, so its 0.982 on Enron in our wave-1 sweep is G3, not zero-shot in any meaningful sense. Its near-chance 0.362 on typed-decisions' unseen workflows is the honest G5 reading of the same model.
  • Jev: training data undisclosed, ungradable; the independently measured out-of-distribution ECE tripling (0.032 → 0.107) is what rung inflation looks like from the outside.
  • Our own wave-1 numbers, re-graded in wave 2: the deberta-zeroshot checkpoints trained on sms_spam, three emotion datasets, two topic datasets, four sentiment datasets, MASSIVE, and Banking77 itself. Under family-level strictness, every suite row is G3 or below for them, and Banking77 is G0. bart-large-mnli (MNLI-only) is the only full-disclosure model with defensible G4 rows across the suite (mean acc 0.390); GLiClass holds five G4* rows (0.529) and Laya three (0.513), both under partial-disclosure caveats. Headline claims may cite G4+ results only; G0–G3 rows remain for comparability and error analysis.

Consequence for our own mixture: a breadth mixture covers most eval families by design, so G4 rows for a mixture-trained model exist only under leave-one-family-out configurations (train without the emotion family, evaluate DailyDialog, and so on). That is ablation I in Section 8.3, and every headline G4 claim comes from a LOFO run.

Section 6Backbone choice at the 1,024–2,048 token target

The context target reshuffles the backbone shortlist, and the architecture-flexibility question has a clear evidence-based answer as of September 2026.

DeBERTa-v3 loses its lead once the window matters

DeBERTa-v3 is a native-512 model. Its relative position embeddings let it run longer, and there is one paper plus practitioner reports of it generalizing to 2,048, but its own zero-shot model cards warn that longer inputs are both slower and worse, it is the slowest encoder in its class (70.2k tokens/s vs. 180.4k for BERT-base in ModernBERT's efficiency table, with no FlashAttention path for its disentangled attention), and no validated 1–2k classification benchmark exists for it. A controlled 2025 study (arXiv:2504.08716) still finds DeBERTaV3 more sample-efficient than ModernBERT at ≤512 when pretraining data is held constant, so it survives in this program as a short-input quality reference in the ablations, not as the primary backbone.

The evidence winner is the ModernBERT family

ModernBERT-large (395M, native 8,192), Ettin-encoder-400m (same lineage, best open-data GLUE at this size: 90.8 vs. ModernBERT-large's 90.4, MIT license), NeoBERT (250M, 4,096), and mmBERT-base (307M, 8,192, multilingual, the Phase 2 candidate) all cover the target window natively. The only independent long-input classification wins in the literature also land here: on genuinely long tasks (legal, long reviews), an 8k-context encoder beat 512-token equivalents by 0.5–2.5 points.

Mamba and SSMs: admissible, currently unearning

Three findings close this out for now. First, there is no latency case at 1–2k tokens: the measured sub-quadratic win over a FlashAttention BERT is about 9% at 1,024 tokens and 47% at 2,048, with the real payoff starting at 4k+, and ModernBERT's unpadding plus local/global attention is a stronger baseline than FA-BERT. Independent reranking work (RankMamba) found pure Mamba slower to train than FlashAttention transformers at these lengths because kernel maturity dominates asymptotics. Second, quality is parity at best: Hydra (bidirectional Mamba-2) beats BERT by 0.8 GLUE points at 112M in its authors' own controlled study, and the single pro-SSM classification datapoint found (essay scoring, Mamba-130m edging ModernBERT) is one dataset with small margins. Third, the ecosystem is empty where this program needs it: no production-grade SSM encoder checkpoints exist (Hydra is one research checkpoint; the M2-BERT retrieval model sees ~768 downloads a month), no zero-shot or instruction-format SSM classifier exists at all, and frozen Mamba features are demonstrably unusable for sentence classification (mean pairwise cosine similarity 0.9999; CoLA MCC 0.000 from the final SSM state).

The recipe stays backbone-agnostic on purpose, so this is a revisit-trigger rather than a door closed: if the context target ever grows past ~4k, hybrid designs (MaBERT interleaves transformer and Mamba layers and reports ~2.4x speedups at 4,096 tokens) become the thing to re-evaluate. The sentence-embedding bi-encoder shape needs no such caveat; it is recipe D, the runner-up, and the strongest embedding models already handle 1–2k inputs.

Longer context is not free accuracy The benefit is task-dependent. One multilingual policy-classification study found a strong 512-token XLM-RoBERTa (0.83 F1) beat a 2,048-token Longformer (0.79), and truncating the Longformer to 512 changed nothing, because the signal was front-loaded. The harness needs at least one genuinely long-input task before we conclude our tasks pay for 2k.

Section 7The dataset plan

Three layers: the training mixture, the held-out evaluation suite, and the public benchmarking layer. Two policies govern all of it: a contamination policy (a reserve list excluded from training by construction, applied per-task to any aggregate we ingest) and a license policy (a commercial-clean core, with documented-risk additions kept separable).

The contamination finding that shapes everything

BTZSC, the community's 22-task zero-shot yardstick (arXiv:2603.11991), collapses to 17 source datasets, and every one of them sits inside Laurer's v1.1 training mixture; tasksource carries most of them too. Public "zero-shot" scores from mixture-trained models are therefore partly memorization, and any suite we assemble carelessly inherits the same problem. Our rule: the reserve list below never enters training, aggregate exports are filtered per task against it (tasksource's label-NLI ships a task column for exactly this; a FLAN subset has to be rebuilt from scratch anyway), and any dataset we ever showcase in a public benchmark joins the list permanently.

Reserved for eval, never trained on: the intent triple (Banking77, CLINC150, HWU64), the classic zero-shot sets (AG News, DBpedia, Yahoo Answers, IMDB, SST-2, amazon_polarity, dair-ai/emotion), the statement-tuning held-outs (Balanced COPA, MRPC, Fig-QA, Story Cloze), the guardrail evals (ToxicChat, JailbreakBench, the OpenAI moderation eval, the wildguardtest split), Belebele, everything in BTZSC, and MMLU/BBH.

The skeleton starts from the one proven commercial-clean core (Laurer's "-c" recipe, reused as-is by Political DEBATE: MNLI + FEVER-NLI + Apache-licensed synthetic) and adds curated families per decision primitive. Synthetic generation route. OpenAI's business terms carry a Permitted Exception that names this project's shape verbatim: customers may "develop artificial intelligence models primarily intended to categorize, classify, or organize data (e.g., embeddings or classifiers), if these models are not distributed or made commercially available to third parties." That is the cleanest route while the model stays internal, and it dies the day we distribute weights. For a distributable model, use generators whose licenses attach no strings to outputs: Qwen3, Mixtral, Mistral Small (Apache 2.0), DeepSeek-R1, Phi-4 (MIT); Gemma explicitly claims no rights in outputs; Llama attaches naming obligations; the Anthropic and Google APIs have no classifier carve-out. This makes "internal-only or distributable?" a Phase-1 decision, added to the open questions.

How much actually needs generating (superseded by the no-LLM-synthetic revision below; kept as the accounting that informed it). By provenance, the original Phase-1 mixture was roughly 60% human-labeled public data (~1.1M rows), 37% existing Apache-licensed synthetic corpora reused as-is (~700k rows, generated by Mixtral and others before we arrived), and only ~3% new generation (~50k rows: the ~6 domain-decision families at ~5k each, ordinal regeneration where public sets are license-poor, and factual-consistency regeneration where the public set is non-commercial). The 3% understates its weight: per the statement-tuning ablation, task-family count drives zero-shot transfer, and the new slice is the only place the model meets its actual target domain. The generator also has three jobs beyond those rows: labeling real unlabeled prod traces (recipe E), soft labels via logprobs for distillation, and template paraphrasing. Phase 2 adds an unavoidable second bill (multilingual ordinal ~10–20k, multilingual guardrails/groundedness ~20–30k) with no public alternative.

What the open reproductions actually used (provenance reviewed 2026-09-21). Kev fine-tunes Qwen3.5 with LoRA on ~14k examples: 1,000 each from ten public sets (Banking77, BoolQ, AG News, MNLI, SST-5, Yelp, TREC, DBpedia-14, the withdrawn Amazon multilingual reviews, IMDB) plus ~4k procedurally generated records (policy minimal pairs over nine template families, 60 random rule structures rendered in four styles, and a delta pass of date-bearing policy cases), with no LLM named anywhere in its generation pipeline. Laya's base training remains undisclosed beyond the author's "100% human labels" claim; its typed-decisions fine-tune trains on the benchmark's own 1,200-case split with soft cross-entropy against gold distributions the benchmark card attributes to an unnamed "teacher endpoint" (three samples at temperature 0.7, averaged), so the LLM dependency passes through the benchmark rather than Laya's code. The Doom writeup discloses nothing about its fine-tuning data. Three consequences for us: no reproduction shows a hard OpenAI dependency; Kev proves the policy and rule families can be generated procedurally with no LLM at all, a third route alongside API and open-model generation; and Kev's mixture is a cautionary tale, both contaminated against the community eval canon (five of its ten sets are standard zero-shot evals) and license-dirty (withdrawn MARC, Yelp terms, non-commercial IMDB/AG News). The reserve-list and license discipline above is a differentiator, not pedantry.

Revision: the no-LLM-synthetic mixture (adopted 2026-09-21)

With the decision to avoid LLM-synthetic training data, the mixture drops its three synthetic backbones (synthetic_zeroshot_mixtral, gliclass-v2.0, the refinedweb-categories slice) and the OpenAI generation route entirely. What remains is the ~1.1M-row human core: the NLI backbone, the task-filtered label-NLI aggregate (itself built from human datasets), and the capped per-primitive families, roughly 30 task families. The evidence says this is viable rather than a sacrifice: Laurer's v1.1 models, statement-tuning, and UniMC are all human-data recipes, the +9.4% zero-shot jump came from a 52k human classification slice, and Laya's author claims the same human-only stance for its base checkpoints.

Three consequences. First, the domain-decision, policy, and robustness families switch to procedural generation in Kev's style (template-rendered policy minimal pairs, random rule structures rendered in multiple styles, and evidence-removed controls whose correct answer is uncertainty), which involves no LLM and no license analysis. Second, the open-domain topic family loses its only clean large source (the synthetic categories slice); topic breadth now comes from SIB-200's English slice, 20 newsgroups, Manifesto, CAP SotU, and MultiEURLEX-en, which is thinner but adequate at the 5k-per-dataset cap. Third, the policy's precise line (ruled 2026-09-21): no LLM-produced labels; LLM-drafted text with human review and human labels is acceptable. WANLI (103k pairs, GPT-3-drafted, human-revised and human-labeled, CC-BY-4.0) therefore stays in the NLI backbone and leaves our eval pool. The pipeline that results has zero API dependency, which also makes an eventual open-source release clean by construction.

Protocol, assembled from the three live traditions: frozen dataset revisions, prompts, and seeds before any test inference (the elcronos precedent); few-shot curves at K ∈ {10, 25, 50, 100} per option with at least 5 resamples reporting mean ± std (the SetFit/FewMany hybrid); calibration reported as 15-bin equal-width ECE for cross-paper comparability plus debiased ECE, Brier, and NLL as the primary internal metrics; latency reported separately for local batch-1 and hosted end-to-end. Because these models are deterministic forward passes, worst/best/mean is defined over perturbations rather than repeated identical calls: bootstrap confidence intervals over eval examples, min/mean/max over the few-shot resamples, M option-order shuffles per example (flip rate plus min-accuracy; option reordering flips 13% of Jev's choices), and 3+ question phrasings per task (a badly-worded criteria description drove Jev below the random floor). BTZSC, Banking77, MASSIVE, and AG News run as comparability-only, permanently flagged.

The public benchmarking layer

One harness, three outputs. The static suite above produces the tables; the same per-decision logs render the visual artifact (reliability diagrams with bin counts, per-example option-probability views, latency-vs-label-count curves, option-order flip rates); and one closed-loop episodic scenario covers what static suites cannot: sequential decisions compounding while the environment moves, in-domain (agent-trace routing and escalation episodes) rather than Doom, with 50+ seeds, confidence intervals, every episode published, and an adapter-phrasing ablation. Anything showcased publicly joins the reserve list.

The second gap is exactly the guard-and-verification slice a survey-analytics assistant needs, so it goes first in the synthetic queue.

Section 8The experimental program

Purpose: determine whether there is a genuinely interesting research result here before any model is built for release. The positioning is deliberately modest, and stays that way regardless of results: the question is how much usable decision capability existing encoder technology yields with better training, better decision conditioning, and much less task-specific supervision. Every design below gives the proposed architecture a fair chance to lose, and a falsifying result is reported as the finding, not buried.

8.2 Candidate-set experiments: K as a first-class variable

The model's contract is state + question + candidate options → distribution over options, so K is a primary experimental variable, not an implementation detail.

  • K sweep at 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024 via a nested-distractor design: merge a real label universe (CLINC-151, Banking77, HWU64, MASSIVE-60, DBpedia-L2-70, Amazon-106, deduplicated to ~400–500 options; extended toward 1,024 with product and category taxonomies), hold each item's gold option fixed, and grow the distractor set. Same items at every K, so the K effect is not confounded with item difficulty.
  • Conditions: seen options, unseen options, recombinations of seen options (the G1/G2 probes); random option permutations (flip rate and min-accuracy); paraphrased option wording; length-matched options to guard against length heuristics.
  • Metrics vs. K: accuracy, top-3/top-5, NLL, Brier, ECE, and calibration-vs-K curves.
  • The physical ceiling, stated up front: at ~4 tokens per option, K=512 costs roughly 2k tokens of options alone, which exceeds the input budget before any state is included. Near K ≈ 300–400 the single-pass claim ends at a 2,048-token budget; beyond it we compare sharded scoring (softmax merged over option chunks, cost linear again) against an embedding prefilter (A2 recall stage feeding an A3 rerank). If the architecture only works for small K, this experiment says so with numbers.

8.3 Ablations

  1. A — Pretrained language knowledge: the same A3 head and mixture on ModernBERT-large vs. an older-generation encoder (RoBERTa-large) vs. from-scratch initialization. The ModernBERT-Instruct precedent says this can flip results.
  2. B — Decision-format pretraining: backbone + head with the multi-task decision mixture vs. without it. UniMC's 78.6 → 38.4 collapse is the predicted direction; this measures it for A3 at this scale.
  3. C — Option-conditioned architecture: A3 vs. A1 vs. A2 trained on the same mixture with the same objective. The central test of whether the proposed architecture earns anything.
  4. D — Variable-K training: mixture sampled at fixed K=4 vs. mixed K ∈ {2..64}, then evaluate the full K curve. Tests whether variable-cardinality behavior is trained or free.
  5. E — Contrastive stage: with and without a SetFit-style contrastive warm-up.
  6. F — Proper-scoring loss vs. CE, differentiable, no RL.
  7. G — RLCD vs. F: the same reward optimized by REINFORCE-plus-noise vs. direct gradients. F ≈ G within CI means RLCD is unnecessary; Jev's use of it is not evidence.
  8. H — Supervision source: human-only vs. + procedural vs. + LLM-generated decision data (research-only arm, never in a release).
  9. I — Leave-one-family-out (the G4 instrument): the breadth mixture covers most eval families, so genuine G4 measurement requires retraining with one family held out per configuration (no emotion family → evaluate DailyDialog; no ordinal sentiment → SST-5 and ASAP; no intent → CLINC). Wave-2 rung data made this non-optional: on the current suite, the strongest public zero-shot checkpoints have zero defensible G4 rows.

8.4 Falsification tests

Pre-registered ways for the hypothesis to die:

  1. Architecture unnecessary: A1 (NLI + softmax normalization) trained on the same mixture matches A3 within bootstrap CI on the G4+ suite at every label budget → the option-conditioned design adds nothing but a latency shape.
  2. Few-shot efficiency not novel: A2 + contrastive (SetFit protocol) matches A3 at every budget ≤ 100 labels/option on at least 5 of 6 suite tasks → the few-shot claim belongs to embeddings.
  3. Universality unearned: A0 with 50 labels/option plus temperature beats zero-shot A3 and 10-shot A3 on every task → per-task classifiers with decent calibration were enough.
  4. Cardinality collapse: accuracy at K=128 falls below half its K=16 value, or below A1 at the same K → the variable-cardinality claim fails. The curve gets published either way.
  5. Transfer illusion: A3's advantage exists at G0–G3 and vanishes at G4+ → decision conditioning improved memorization-adjacent transfer, not generality.
  6. Calibration not architectural: post-temperature ECE indistinguishable across A1/A2/A3 at matched accuracy → calibration is a training and temperature property, and drops out of the architecture argument.

8.5 Metrics and methodology

Accuracy, top-3 and top-5 accuracy, NLL, Brier, 15-bin equal-width ECE plus a debiased estimator and an equal-mass-binning check, reliability diagrams with bin counts, calibration-vs-K curves, and accuracy over confidence slices. The ECE numbers in Section 11 (0.10 zero-shot, 0.05 few-shot) are engineering targets, never scientific cutoffs; models are compared only under identical binning, identical subsets, and identical seeds, with the methodology stated on every table. Frozen revisions and seeds before headline runs; worst/best/mean is defined over perturbations and resamples per the Section 7 protocol.

8.6 Recommended pipeline for the ~400M prototype

Backbone: ModernBERT-large or Ettin-encoder-400m (both 8k-native; Ettin has the best open-data GLUE at this size), with the ablation grid run at ~150M (ModernBERT-base / Ettin-150m) for cost and the winning configurations re-run at 400M. Head: option-marker scoring (A3), starting from the Laya and kotoba-lang reference implementations. Mixture: Section 7's ~30 human and procedural families, capped at 500/class and 5k/dataset, 3+ templates per task, option-order shuffling, variable-K sampling per ablation D. Objective: start with CE plus per-schema temperature; adopt a proper-scoring loss or RLCD only if ablations F/G show a real gain. Few-shot path: LoRA from the mixture-trained checkpoint at 10–100 labels per option.

8.7 Evidence thresholds and budget

Convincing evidence for a general-purpose decision model: on the G4+-graded suite, the mixture-trained A3 beats deberta-v3-large-zeroshot-v2.0 by a CI-separated margin (≥5 suite-mean accuracy points) and beats Laya's checkpoints; post-temperature calibration at or under the engineering targets under the stated methodology; single-pass latency preserved to K ≈ 128; and few-shot curves that reach the A0-at-500-labels ceiling using ≤100 labels per option. Falsification: any test in 8.4. Anything in between is reported as the mixed result it is.

Rough budget for the first research prototype. Data: ≈ $0 (public human sets plus procedural generation; no API spend under the supervision policy). Compute: the 150M ablation grid (8 arms × ~4–6 A100-hours) ≈ 40–50 hours; two to three full 400M mixture runs ≈ 40–60 hours; the baseline matrix's ~800 small fine-tunes ≈ 30–50 hours; K-sweep and evaluation ≈ 20 hours. On the order of 150–250 A100-hours, roughly $300–600 at current rental prices, with the 150M grid largely feasible on free Kaggle-class GPUs (Laya's own multilingual fine-tune ran 4 epochs in ~5 hours on a free 2×T4). Wall-clock: weeks, not months.

Section 9Reality checks the hype leaves out

  • Zero-shot has a hard ceiling. In the one frozen-protocol comparison covering both Jev and Laya, logistic regression with 500–5,000 labels beat every zero-shot decision system on every dataset. On beri.net's phishing bench, a fine-tuned small model (97.4%, ECE 0.010, 1,000 labels) beat Jev's best decomposed configuration (95.0%). Zero-shot is a cold-start and long-tail feature, not the end state of any specific task.
  • The few-shot gap you attribute to BERT-family models is already closed by published recipes. The 8-examples-per-class regime is solved by SetFit-style contrastive tuning or by adapting a format-trained checkpoint; what vanilla head fine-tuning needs (roughly 500–1,000 labels to decisively beat GPT-4o zero/few-shot on complex multi-class tasks per a 2024 political-science study) is the wrong baseline to generalize from.
  • Both vendors' headline comparisons are methodologically soft. TypeSafe's accuracy benchmark uses averaged frontier-LLM predictions as ground truth and self-authored workflows. Laya's typed-decisions win compares its fine-tuned checkpoint against zero-shot Jev, and its latency number is a local T4 measurement against Jev's hosted API. Independent reruns erased one headline result entirely.
  • Calibration claims need per-schema scrutiny. Laya ships overconfident (raw ECE 0.466) and one shipped temperature produced ECE 0.610 on a 20-label task. Jev rounds probabilities to two decimals, putting exactly zero mass on the true label in 15% of rows on one benchmark, and its mid-range confidences are mushy (the 0.5–0.6 bucket was 38% correct on spam). Trustworthy softmax needs held-out labeled data per schema family, a few hundred examples, and that is a real hidden label cost.
  • PEFT does not reduce label needs. LoRA and adapters cut compute and memory at the same label counts; budget labels independently of the PEFT decision.

Section 10Assumptions and risks

  • Assumes classification-shaped decisions. Everything above holds for choice/score/binary over a declared answer space. Tasks needing counting, arithmetic, date reasoning, or multi-hop composition break these models (documented for Jev; structural for one-pass encoders). The escape hatch is decomposition into atomic questions, which worked dramatically for Jev (62.6% → 95.0%) but shifts complexity into schema design.
  • Assumes English or high-resource languages. Laya's English checkpoint scores 0.000 on Khmer at 0.952 confidence; its multilingual checkpoint is weak on low-resource languages (Swahili 0.210, Amharic 0.110). Multilingual needs push toward mmBERT backbones and cost accuracy on English.
  • Risk: silent overconfident failure out of scope. The failure mode common to the whole class. Confidence gating cannot save you when the model is confidently wrong outside its training distribution; scope routing or script detection has to happen before the forward pass.
  • Risk: self-benchmarking. GLiClass and Laya numbers are mostly vendor-run; the single independent multi-model benchmark (BTZSC) omitted label-token models entirely. Our own harness is not optional.
  • Assumes LLM-synthetic training data remains acceptable. Laurer's and GLiClass's recipes lean on Mixtral/GPT-4o generations; license and quality both need checking if this becomes a product component. Synthetic data degrades on subjective tasks.
  • Not evaluated here: non-text state (images, structured telemetry at scale), regression outputs, and sequence labeling. GLiNER2 covers extraction if that becomes relevant.

Section 11Resolved framing and the calibration bar

Resolved 2026-09-21:

  1. General research first. Potloc use cases (the DA-7547 scope router remains the natural candidate) are a later expansion, not the anchor.
  2. Zero-shot bar: beat Laya's open checkpoints on the held-out suite. Deliberately modest, since the program's headline metric is the fine-tuning curve at 10–100 labels per option. Stretch reference, not a gate: deberta-v3-large-zeroshot-v2.0's 0.676 avg F1, the strongest free non-LLM zero-shot baseline.
  3. No LLM-synthetic training data, precisely: no LLM-produced labels. The rule guards against distilling a teacher's mistakes, so LLM-produced labels and teacher distributions are out (typed-decisions-style gold, GPT-4o-labeled corpora), while LLM-drafted text that humans reviewed and labeled is in. Ruled 2026-09-21: WANLI stays (GPT-3-drafted, human-revised and human-labeled, CC-BY-4.0, standard in the strongest zero-shot recipes); because we train on it, WANLI is permanently excluded from our eval sets. LLM-labeled eval benchmarks (typed-decisions) are kept, flagged as teacher-labeled. Refined by the v2 experimental program: ablation H may use LLM-generated decision data as a research-only comparison arm, measured and reported but excluded from any released model.
  4. Open-source decision deferred until results. Until then the mixture stays license-clean and API-free, which keeps the release option costless.

Calibration: what others achieved, and the proposed bar. The academic zero-shot classifier literature reports no calibration at all across all eight threads surveyed; the numbers that exist come from the 2026 product ecosystem and the supervised literature. Jev, trained with proper-scoring-rule rewards, measures ECE 0.024–0.032 in-distribution but 0.107 out-of-distribution, with per-dataset excursions to 0.281 and a mushy mid-range. Laya ships overconfident (raw ECE 0.466) and reaches 0.081 only after fitting a temperature per question type and option count on held-out data, and one shipped temperature still produced 0.610 on a 20-label task. A conventionally fine-tuned small model with 1,000 labels plus temperature scaling hit ECE 0.010 on the same phishing benchmark both products were tested on. Supervised practice (Guo 2017; Desai & Durrett 2020) says fine-tuned encoders land in the ~0.01–0.05 band in-domain after temperature scaling and degrade out-of-domain. Confirmed bar (engineering targets, not scientific cutoffs): report 15-bin ECE, debiased ECE, Brier, and NLL on every run; aim for post-temperature ECE ≤ 0.10 zero-shot on unseen tasks and ≤ 0.05 after few-shot fine-tuning; models are compared only under identical ECE methodology, stated on every table. RLCD-style scoring-rule training is adopted only if the F/G ablations show temperature scaling alone cannot get there.
